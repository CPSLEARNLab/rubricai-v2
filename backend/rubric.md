# Rubric — Recalibrated April 2026

> **Calibration note:** The scale has been recalibrated for high school students in a micro-internship simulation context. Level 4 (Mastery) reflects advanced-but-achievable performance for a student who has practiced — approximately old L3–L3.5. Level 3 reflects solid developing performance — approximately old L2.5. Level 1 is a thin band reserved for truly minimal or disengaged performance. Level 2 covers clearly-below-competent work that shows some engagement. L3 and L4 together should account for the majority of the distribution for students who meaningfully participated.

---

## Communication

### Cluster 1: Interview Communication

**Research foundation:** This cluster operationalizes interview communication competencies grounded in qualitative research methodology (Patton, 2015; Seidman, 2019) and communication theory. The questioning behaviors align with Kvale & Brinkmann's (2009) typology of open-ended questioning and probing strategies in qualitative interviewing. Active listening is operationalized through Bodie's (2011) Active-Empathic Listening Scale, which identifies three empirically-validated dimensions: sensing (acknowledging the speaker's message), processing (interpreting meaning), and responding (providing verbal and nonverbal feedback). Rapport-building behaviors are grounded in Rogers & Farson's (1957) foundational work on empathic listening and Brown & Levinson's (1987) politeness theory. Communication adaptation draws from Clark's (1996) audience design framework and Giles' (1991) Communication Accommodation Theory, which explain how skilled communicators adjust style based on context and interviewee responses.

**Indicators**
* Asks clear, open-ended questions.
* Uses follow-up questions to probe deeper into ideas or emotions and build depth.
* Demonstrates active listening (paraphrasing, clarifying, summarizing).
* Adjusts communication style to fit the circumstance.
* Introduces self and purpose professionally.
* Builds rapport with stakeholders through tone, curiosity, and responsiveness.

#### Indicator 1: Asks clear, open-ended questions

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Asks 0–1 open-ended questions per 10-minute segment (e.g., "Do you like it?", "How many?"); questions are vague, confusing, or clearly leading (e.g., "Don't you think that...?"); interviewee asks for clarification ≥3 times; questions frequently conflate multiple topics in a single turn (e.g., "What do you think about X and also how do you feel about Y?") |
| Level 2 | Asks 2–3 open-ended questions per 10-minute segment; questions are sometimes vague or confusing (interviewee asks for clarification 1–2 times); some leading phrasing present; questions sometimes conflate topics; questions are not strategically phrased to elicit rich responses |
| Level 3 | Asks 4–5 open-ended questions per 10-minute segment (e.g., "Can you tell me about...?", "What was your experience with...?", "How did you approach...?"); questions are clearly phrased requiring minimal clarification (≤1 request per 10 min); no leading language; questions focus on one topic at a time |
| Level 4 | Asks 5–6 open-ended questions per 10-minute segment; questions are clearly phrased and non-leading; questions focus on one topic at a time; demonstrates strategic phrasing 1–2 times per interview using prompts like "Walk me through...", "Help me understand...", "What stood out to you about...?"; at least 1 question invites storytelling rather than reporting (e.g., "Tell me about a time when..." vs. "What are your thoughts on...?") |

---

#### Indicator 2: Uses follow-up questions to probe deeper into ideas or emotions

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Asks 0–15% follow-up questions relative to total questions asked; identifies and probes 0–15% of key opportunities including: emotional words (frustration, excitement), contradictions between statements, vague terms ("things," "stuff"), or hedging language ("kind of," "sort of"); when interviewee provides a surface answer, moves to next question without any probing; no follow-up on follow-up response |
| Level 2 | Asks 16–35% follow-up questions; identifies and probes 16–40% of key opportunities; follow-ups are generic without referencing specific content from what was just said; pursues depth through 1-level probing only (asks one follow-up to the initial response but does not follow up on the follow-up) |
| Level 3 | Asks 36–55% follow-up questions; identifies and probes 41–60% of key opportunities; follow-ups directly reference the interviewee's statements (e.g., "You mentioned 'challenging'—what specifically was challenging about it?"); follow-ups extend beyond the initial response (e.g., "What made it challenging?" → "How did you handle that challenge?"); pursues depth through 2-level probing in at least one exchange |
| Level 4 | Asks 55–65% follow-up questions; identifies and probes 60–75% of key opportunities; explicitly names what they're probing at least once (e.g., "I'm curious about the frustration you mentioned—tell me more about that"); pursues 2-level probing in more than one exchange; uses at least 2 different probe types from: emotion probes ("What was that like for you?"), example probes ("Can you give me a specific instance?"), contrast probes ("How is this different from...?"), or assumption probes ("What makes you think that?") |

#### Indicator 3: Demonstrates active listening (paraphrasing, clarifying, summarizing)

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | All of the following are true: student never restates, paraphrases, or summarizes anything the interviewee said; student never asks a question that references something the interviewee just said; student moves to a new topic immediately after each interviewee response without any acknowledgment; student interrupts the interviewee 3 or more times |
| Level 2 | At least 2 of the following are present: does not interrupt (0–1 interruptions across the full interview); pauses after interviewee responses before asking the next question — visible in transcript as a natural gap rather than an immediate pivot; uses brief verbal acknowledgments that reference what was just said, not just generic "okay" or "mm-hmm" — e.g., "I see, so the menu was the issue" or "right, the convenience factor"; mirrors the interviewee's tone — if interviewee expresses frustration, student acknowledges it ("that sounds frustrating") rather than moving on |
| Level 3 | All of the following are true: does not interrupt (0–1 interruptions across the full interview); pauses after interviewee responses before asking the next question; uses brief verbal acknowledgments that reference what was just said, not just generic "okay" or "mm-hmm" — e.g., "I see, so the menu was the issue" or "right, the convenience factor"; mirrors the interviewee's tone — if interviewee expresses frustration, student acknowledges it ("that sounds frustrating") rather than moving on; PLUS at least 1 of the following: **Pattern-naming:** student explicitly names a theme they've noticed across multiple interviewee responses during the interview itself — e.g., "I'm noticing that convenience keeps coming up" or "It sounds like the menu is a recurring issue for you" — must reference at least two prior interviewee statements, not just the most recent one; **Deepening clarifying question:** student asks a question that shows they processed the content deeply enough to know what they still don't understand — e.g., "Help me make sure I understand — are you saying the menu has too many items, or that the items aren't labeled clearly enough?" — the clarifying question must show the student has a partial understanding and is checking it, not simply asking for more information |
| Level 4 | All of the following are true: does not interrupt (0–1 interruptions across the full interview); pauses after interviewee responses before asking the next question; uses brief verbal acknowledgments that reference what was just said; mirrors the interviewee's tone when relevant; names at least one pattern or theme heard across multiple interviewee responses — e.g., "I'm noticing that convenience keeps coming up" or "It sounds like the menu is a recurring issue for you" — must reference at least two prior interviewee statements; asks at least one deepening clarifying question that shows partial understanding and checks it — e.g., "Help me make sure I understand — are you saying the menu has too many items, or that the items aren't labeled clearly enough?"; PLUS at least 1 of the following: **Interpretive paraphrase:** student restates what the interviewee said AND adds their own interpretation of what it means — e.g., "It sounds like the core issue is that you don't feel confident you're making the right choice, which makes the whole experience feel less satisfying" — the interpretation clause ("which means...", "which suggests...", "so the real issue is...") must be present; a simple accurate restatement without added interpretation does not count; **Thematic summary:** student provides a summary that groups multiple things the interviewee said into named themes and explains how they connect — e.g., "So you've described two main things: the menu is hard to navigate, and the food quality is inconsistent — and it sounds like both of those make it hard to feel like you're getting good value" — must cover at least 2 distinct themes and name the relationship between them |

#### Indicator 4: Adjusts communication style to fit the circumstance

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Makes 0 adaptations per 10-minute segment; uses uniform communication style throughout (same vocabulary level, sentence complexity, tone, and level of detail); does not respond to interviewee signals of confusion or requests for clarification |
| Level 2 | Makes 1–2 adaptations per 10-minute segment; adaptations are reactive (occur only after interviewee explicitly signals confusion or asks for clarification); uses single-dimension adjustments only (e.g., simplifies vocabulary OR adds example OR changes tone, but adjusts only one aspect at a time) |
| Level 3 | Makes 3–4 adaptations per 10-minute segment; adaptations are responsive (occur when interviewee shows confusion, not only when they explicitly ask); uses multi-dimension adjustments in at least one instance (e.g., simplifies vocabulary AND provides a concrete example; or adjusts tone AND level of detail); adaptations align with interviewee needs and improve clarity |
| Level 4 | Makes 4–5 adaptations per 10-minute segment; at least one is proactive (anticipates a need before confusion is visible, based on interviewee role, background, or subtle cues); at least once uses meta-communication to signal the adjustment (e.g., "Let me simplify this," "Let me be more specific," "Given what you said, let me try asking this differently"); adaptations consistently improve clarity rather than just varying style |

#### Indicator 5: Introduces self and purpose professionally

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Introduction is absent or clearly incomplete; omits 2 or more of the essential elements (name, role, purpose); listener cannot determine why the interaction is occurring, what the speaker's role is, or what will happen next |
| Level 2 | Introduction includes name, role, and purpose but presents them as disconnected facts without explaining why they matter for the listener (e.g., "I'm Sarah, I work on Project X, we're doing interviews") — no relevance framing; information is listed rather than organized |
| Level 3 | Clearly introduces self, role, and purpose in a logically ordered sequence; purpose is explicitly linked to the interaction or stakeholder context so the listener understands why this conversation is happening |
| Level 4 | Includes all Level 3 elements plus at least one of the following: sets expectations for the interaction (e.g., "This will take about 10 minutes, I'll ask about your experience, and your insights will inform our recommendations"); explains the value of the stakeholder's participation (e.g., "Your insights will directly inform our recommendations"); frames purpose in a way that is engaging rather than purely transactional; introduction is concise (typically <45 seconds) despite being comprehensive |

#### Indicator 6: Builds rapport through tone, curiosity, and responsiveness

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Demonstrates 0 rapport-building behaviors per 10-minute segment; interaction is transactional or disengaged: provides no verbal acknowledgment of stakeholder contributions (no "mm-hmm," "I see," "thank you," or validation statements); does not reference or build on stakeholder's prior statements; does not respond to emotional cues in stakeholder's language (e.g., stakeholder says "frustrated" or "excited," interviewer does not acknowledge) |
| Level 2 | Demonstrates 1–2 rapport-building behaviors per 10-minute segment; uses basic verbal acknowledgments (e.g., "I see," "okay," "thank you"); acknowledgments are generic and do not validate or build on specific content (e.g., "That's interesting" without specifying what is interesting); minimal emotional responsiveness (acknowledges emotional language only when very explicit); responses show basic courtesy but do not build on stakeholder input |
| Level 3 | Demonstrates 3–4 rapport-building behaviors per 10-minute segment across multiple dimensions: provides specific verbal validation (e.g., "That's a great point about X" or "That makes a lot of sense"); references stakeholder's prior statements with specificity (e.g., "Earlier you mentioned Y, and now you're saying Z—how do these connect?"); acknowledges emotional content (e.g., when stakeholder uses "frustrating," interviewer says "I hear that was frustrating"); uses verbal encouragers consistently (e.g., "mm-hmm," "tell me more," "I'm curious about that"); stakeholder responses show visible engagement (elaborates beyond what was directly asked) |
| Level 4 | Demonstrates 4–5 rapport-building behaviors per 10-minute segment with strategic sophistication: integrates stakeholder's own language or framing into at least one follow-up question (e.g., stakeholder says "frustrating," interviewer later asks "You mentioned frustration—tell me more about that"); finds or names common ground (e.g., "That makes a lot of sense given...", "That resonates with me given..."); uses verbal reframing to deepen understanding (e.g., "So what I'm hearing is X—is that right?"); explicitly names patterns or connections across stakeholder's statements (e.g., "I'm noticing that time constraints come up in both contexts you've described"); stakeholder provides progressively longer or more elaborated responses across the interview |

### Cluster 2: Communicating Ideas Clearly

**Research Foundation:** This cluster operationalizes oral communication competencies centered on clarity, organization, and audience adaptation. Organizational structure draws from Minto's (2009) Pyramid Principle, which establishes the claim → support → implication structure, and Graesser et al.'s (1994) cognitive research showing that logical sequencing and explicit transitions enhance listener comprehension. The evidence-to-claim connection is grounded in Toulmin's (1958) argument model. Kuhn's (1999) developmental model of critical thinking informs the progression from unsupported assertions to evidence-based reasoning. Professional tone and concision align with PLAIN guidelines. Audience adaptation behaviors are operationalized through Schriver's (1997) framework for audience-centered framing and clarity.

**References**
* Graesser, A. C., Singer, M., & Trabasso, T. (1994). Constructing inferences during narrative text comprehension. *Psychological Review, 101*(3), 371–395.
* Minto, B. (2009). *The pyramid principle: Logic in writing and thinking* (3rd ed.). Pearson Education.
* Toulmin, S. E. (2003). *The uses of argument* (Updated ed.). Cambridge University Press.
* Kuhn, D. (1999). A developmental model of critical thinking. *Educational Researcher, 28*(2), 16–46.
* Plain Language Action and Information Network (PLAIN). Federal plain language guidelines. https://www.plainlanguage.gov/guidelines/
* Schriver, K. A. (1997). *Dynamics in document design: Creating text for readers*. Wiley.
* AAC&U. (2009). Oral Communication VALUE Rubric.

**Indicators**
* Organizes ideas logically with clear transitions.
* Connects claims to evidence during communication.
* Uses a clear, concise, professional tone.
* Adapts message for different audiences (peer vs. client vs. facilitator).

---

#### Indicator 1: Organizes ideas logically with clear transitions

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Ideas appear in arbitrary order with no discernible logic (e.g., jumps from a recommendation to a background fact to an unrelated observation without explanation); each idea stands alone with no connection to what precedes or follows it; listener cannot follow the reasoning arc; conclusions (if any) do not follow from what was said before (e.g., "The cafe needs better signage. Also the food was good. We should hire more staff.") |
| Level 2 | Ideas show basic sequencing — either chronological ("first we asked... then we found...") or simple grouping (puts related ideas together); the logic connecting ideas is implicit: listener can follow the topic but must infer why ideas appear in the order they do; no bridging between groups of ideas; transitions between topics are abrupt even if ideas within a topic are ordered (e.g., "So the customer mentioned the menu was confusing. Also, the seating was uncomfortable. And they said service was slow.") |
| Level 3 | Ideas unfold in a logical sequence where each point builds on or prepares for the next; a recognizable organizational logic is evident — problem → evidence → implication, or general → specific, or observation → interpretation — even if not explicitly named; listener can follow both the topic and the reasoning path without having to infer connections (e.g., "The main issue we heard was confusion at the ordering station. The customer said she didn't know where to stand or what to do first. That suggests the problem isn't the menu itself — it's the physical layout of the space.") |
| Level 4 | Ideas form a coherent argument arc with a clear through-line from opening observation to conclusion; student explicitly connects how earlier points inform later ones, showing awareness of the cumulative structure (e.g., "We started by asking about her overall experience, which led us to the ordering confusion, and that's what led us to our recommendation about signage"); no significant logical gaps; if a new idea is introduced, the student explains where it fits in the overall picture |

---

#### Indicator 2: Connects claims to evidence during communication

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Makes claims without supporting evidence (0–20% of major claims supported); statements are opinion-based or unsupported assertions (e.g., "This is the best approach" without explaining why); when evidence is mentioned, it appears disconnected from the claim (lists facts without explaining relevance) |
| Level 2 | Supports 21–40% of major claims with evidence; connections between claims and evidence are implicit or weak (says "for example" but doesn't explain how the example supports the claim; provides data without interpretation); evidence is relevant but the relationship to the claim requires listener inference |
| Level 3 | Supports 41–60% of major claims with relevant evidence; explicitly connects evidence to claims using reasoning statements (e.g., "This shows that...", "This demonstrates...", "Because X occurred, we can conclude Y"); makes clear why the evidence is relevant and how it supports the claim |
| Level 4 | Supports 55–70% of major claims with relevant evidence; explains not only that evidence supports the claim but how (makes the link explicit rather than assumed); for at least one claim, either addresses a limitation or alternative interpretation of the evidence (e.g., "While this data point suggests X, we should note that...") or explains why the evidence is particularly meaningful in this context |

---

#### Indicator 3: Uses a clear, concise, professional tone

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Speech is difficult to follow: ideas trail off mid-sentence, restart frequently, or collapse into single-word responses when explanation is needed (e.g., "So the thing is... like... it was just... yeah, the cafe."); language is imprecise with frequent vague referents (≥5 uses of "thing," "stuff," "it" without clear antecedent per 10 minutes); register is inappropriate for a professional context: language includes slang or informal expressions (e.g., "yeah so like the place was kinda whatever") OR uses unexplained technical terms that obscure meaning |
| Level 2 | Ideas are understandable but delivery is uneven: student completes most thoughts but loses clarity under pressure or when moving between points; language is clear but includes 2–4 instances of imprecise wording or vague referents per 10 minutes; register is generally appropriate with 1–2 lapses — occasional informal phrasing or unexplained jargon that briefly breaks professional register (e.g., "the customers were basically just annoyed and stuff") |
| Level 3 | Ideas are expressed clearly and completely: student states a point, provides a brief explanation, and lands the thought without trailing off or restarting; language is precise with <2 vague referents per 10 minutes; register is consistently professional throughout: word choice is appropriate for a client-facing context, no unexplained jargon, no informal expressions |
| Level 4 | Ideas are expressed with economy and precision: student conveys the essential point without unnecessary qualification or repetition, and the listener never has to work to extract meaning; language is precise and economical (uses specific nouns/verbs rather than adjective-heavy descriptions; says "deadline" not "time-related pressure situation"); register is audience-calibrated: word choice and level of specificity adjust visibly based on who is being addressed — simpler framing for general points, precise terminology when discussing findings (e.g., "the core issue, in plain terms, is that customers don't know where to start") |

---

#### Indicator 4: Adapts message for different audiences

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Same framing, terminology, and detail level regardless of audience role or context; no visible adjustment when speaking to a peer, a facilitator, or a simulated client |
| Level 2 | Limited or surface-level adjustments (tone only, e.g., slightly more formal); core framing and vocabulary largely unchanged across audiences or contexts |
| Level 3 | Adjusts framing, terminology, or emphasis appropriately based on audience role, goals, or knowledge level; the adjustment is visible and deliberate (e.g., explains jargon when speaking to a non-specialist, increases specificity when speaking to someone with domain knowledge) |
| Level 4 | Strategically reframes messages across audiences; at least once explicitly anticipates what the listener needs to know or decide and shapes the message accordingly (e.g., "For the owner's purposes, the key thing is..." or "In plain terms, what this means is..."); adjusts not just vocabulary but the framing and emphasis of the core message based on who is listening |

## Critical Thinking

### Cluster 1: Information Analysis

**Research Foundation:** The questioning framework draws from King's (1995) work on strategic questioning and Graesser & Person's (1994) taxonomy of question types in knowledge construction. Assumption and gap identification operationalizes Facione's (1990) Delphi Report consensus definition of analysis and Paul & Elder's (2006) critical thinking elements framework. Information evaluation and integration aligns with the AAC&U (2009) Critical Thinking VALUE Rubric's "Evidence" dimension and Walraven et al.'s (2009) research on information credibility assessment. Problem decomposition is grounded in Jonassen's (1997) research on problem-solving in ill-structured domains and the OECD PISA (2012) problem-solving framework.

**References**
* King, A. (1995). Designing the instructional process to enhance critical thinking across the curriculum. *Teaching of Psychology, 22*(1), 13–17.
* Graesser, A. C., & Person, N. K. (1994). Question asking during tutoring. *American Educational Research Journal, 31*(1), 104–137.
* Facione, P. A. (1990). *Critical thinking: A statement of expert consensus* (The Delphi Report). California Academic Press.
* Paul, R., & Elder, L. (2006). *The miniature guide to critical thinking: Concepts and tools*. Foundation for Critical Thinking.
* AAC&U. (2009). Critical Thinking VALUE Rubric.
* Jonassen, D. H. (1997). Instructional design models for well-structured and ill-structured problem-solving learning outcomes. *Educational Technology Research and Development, 45*(1), 65–94.
* OECD. (2014). PISA 2012 Results: Creative Problem Solving (Volume V).

**Indicators**
* Asks clear and targeted questions to explore issues.
* Identifies assumptions, gaps, or contradictions.
* Evaluates relevance of information.
* Breaks down complex problems into manageable parts.

---

#### Indicator 1: Asks clear and targeted questions to explore issues

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Asks 0–1 task-relevant questions per 10-minute segment; questions are broad, generic, or unclear (e.g., "What about this?" "Can you tell me more?" without specifying what), not tied to specific problem elements; does not follow up on responses or pursue clarification when information is vague or incomplete; no identifiable logical sequence |
| Level 2 | Asks 2–3 relevant questions per 10-minute segment; questions address the problem but lack focus (multiple questions cover similar ground, or jump between unrelated topics without completing exploration of any single topic); includes 1–2 follow-up questions but they are reactive rather than purposeful (asks "Why?" or "Can you explain?" without clear purpose); question sequence shows limited logical progression |
| Level 3 | Asks 4–5 relevant and clearly worded questions per 10-minute segment tied to the problem; includes 3–4 follow-ups that move from general to specific (e.g., "What's the issue?" → "What causes it?" → "How does it affect X?"); question sequence generally reflects a logical inquiry path (context → detail → implication); avoids redundant questions |
| Level 4 | Asks 5–6 high-value, targeted questions per 10-minute segment that systematically explore the issue; includes 4–5 follow-ups that test understanding or probe mechanisms (e.g., "If X is the constraint, what happens if we change Y?" OR "You said both A and B are problems — which is the root cause?"); demonstrates at least 1 of the following: asks a meta-question about the inquiry (e.g., "What's most important to know?"); explicitly prioritizes a high-leverage question over a peripheral topic |

#### Indicator 2: Identifies assumptions, gaps, or contradictions

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Identifies 0 assumptions, gaps, or contradictions; overlooks clear contradictions or missing information; does not notice when an answer is incomplete or internally inconsistent |
| Level 2 | Identifies 1–2 gaps or assumptions but descriptions are vague or incomplete; may notice a contradiction when it is very explicit but does not identify them independently; does not articulate why the gap matters for the problem |
| Level 3 | Identifies 2–3 specific assumptions, ambiguities, or contradictions; uses targeted clarification questions to resolve them (e.g., "You mentioned X, but is it safe to assume Y?" or "What information are we missing about Z?"); briefly explains why at least one assumption or gap matters for the problem |
| Level 4 | Identifies 3–4 specific assumptions, gaps, or contradictions that materially affect the problem; demonstrates at least 2 of the following: distinguishes critical gaps from minor ones; tests an assumption explicitly (e.g., "If we assumed Y instead, would our conclusion change?"); clarifies implications (e.g., "If this is wrong, then..."); identifies what information would resolve a key uncertainty |

#### Indicator 3: Evaluates relevance of information

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Questions follow a predetermined sequence regardless of what the customer says — new information received does not visibly influence what gets asked next; all customer responses are treated as equally useful: student pursues tangential or irrelevant content with the same effort as directly relevant content (e.g., customer mentions they visited once two years ago and student spends three questions on the timeline rather than the experience); when a customer gives a vague or thin answer, student accepts it and moves on without attempting to get something more substantive (e.g., Customer: "It was fine." Student: "Okay, and what did you think about the menu?") |
| Level 2 | Student occasionally adjusts based on what the customer says but adjustments are driven by surface features rather than analytical relevance — follows up on what was most recent or most dramatic rather than most useful for understanding the problem; some tangential content is pursued while clearly relevant content is passed over; when a customer gives a vague answer, student makes one attempt to get more but accepts a second vague answer without further pursuit (e.g., Customer: "It was fine." Student: "Can you say more?" Customer: "Just, you know, fine." Student: "Okay, what about the seating?") |
| Level 3 | Student visibly pursues information most relevant to the client problem and moves past content that does not bear on it — question choices reflect a working sense of what the interview needs to produce (e.g., customer mentions both the music and the ordering confusion; student follows up on ordering confusion and moves past the music); when a customer gives a vague or insufficient answer, student probes specifically to get something more substantive rather than accepting the thin response (e.g., Customer: "It was just kind of confusing." Student: "What specifically felt confusing — was it finding where to order, or knowing what to order, or something else?"); student redirects away from tangential content back toward the problem when the conversation drifts (e.g., "That's interesting — I want to make sure we also cover what the actual ordering experience was like for you") |
| Level 4 | Student distinguishes between different types of information and treats them accordingly — separates the customer's direct experience from their opinions or recommendations, and redirects opinion back toward experience when it would be more useful (e.g., Customer: "You should add more seating." Student: "Tell me about a time when seating was actually a problem for you — what happened?"); student visibly weighs the sufficiency of information received and probes further when an answer is analytically incomplete, even if it was fluently delivered (e.g., "You mentioned the experience felt disorganized — I want to make sure I understand that well enough. Was that more about the physical space, or the staff, or the process of ordering itself?"); when something unexpected or potentially contradictory emerges, student flags it and pursues it rather than passing over it (e.g., "You said you'd come back, but earlier you mentioned the experience was frustrating — I'm curious about that, because those two things seem like they're in tension") |

---

#### Indicator 4: Breaks down complex problems into manageable parts

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Describes problem globally without breaking it into parts (e.g., "The problem is everything isn't working" OR "Users don't like it"); does not identify or name distinct components; when components are mentioned, they are not organized or categorized (lists items without grouping or structure) |
| Level 2 | Accurately identifies 1–2 components but omits key elements (e.g., "There are issues with cost and timeline"); organization is a simple list without explaining how components relate or which matter most; omits at least one major component that should be evident from the conversation |
| Level 3 | Identifies 3–4 distinct components organized by a clear framework (e.g., "The problem breaks down by stakeholder: users need X, administrators need Y, IT needs Z" OR "There are three types of constraints: time, budget, and technical"); explains how 2–3 components relate to each other or to the overall problem; components cover the major aspects without critical gaps |
| Level 4 | Identifies 4–5 coherent components that comprehensively map the problem; structure is optimized for analysis (causal sequence, thematic grouping, or similar); demonstrates at least 2 of the following: explicitly prioritizes components by impact or leverage (e.g., "X is most critical because addressing it impacts Y and Z"); distinguishes what is addressable vs. external constraints; shows dependencies between components (e.g., "We must solve X before we can address Y"); acknowledges where the analysis is limited by available information |

### Cluster 2: Insight Synthesis

**Research Foundation:** Pattern recognition and theme identification draw from Chi, Feltovich, & Glaser's (1981) research on expert-novice differences in categorization and from Braun & Clarke's (2006) thematic analysis methodology, which establishes that identifying 2–3 semantic themes represents competent analysis while identifying 4–5+ themes including latent patterns represents advanced analytical sophistication. Evidence synthesis operationalizes the AAC&U (2009) Critical Thinking VALUE Rubric's "Conclusions" dimension and Bloom's Revised Taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001). The evidence → insight → implication reasoning chain is grounded in Toulmin's (2003) argumentation model and Paul & Elder's (2006) critical thinking framework.

**References**
* Chi, M. T. H., Feltovich, P. J., & Glaser, R. (1981). Categorization and representation of physics problems by experts and novices. *Cognitive Science, 5*(2), 121–152.
* Braun, V., & Clarke, V. (2006). Using thematic analysis in psychology. *Qualitative Research in Psychology, 3*(2), 77–101.
* Anderson, L. W., & Krathwohl, D. R. (Eds.). (2001). *A taxonomy for learning, teaching, and assessing*. Longman.
* AAC&U. (2009). Critical Thinking VALUE Rubric.
* Toulmin, S. E. (2003). *The uses of argument* (Updated ed.). Cambridge University Press.
* Paul, R., & Elder, L. (2006). *The miniature guide to critical thinking: Concepts and tools*. Foundation for Critical Thinking.

**Indicators**
* Identifies relationships across data points.
* Recognizes patterns, themes, and root causes.
* Synthesizes evidence into insights and implications.
* Organizes insights with clear logical structure.

---

#### Indicator 1: Identifies relationships across data points

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Makes 0 explicit connections between pieces of information during conversation; describes each data point separately without showing how they relate (e.g., "Person A said X. Person B said Y." with no connection stated); when asked how information relates, cannot articulate relationships or says "I don't know" |
| Level 2 | Makes 1–2 basic connections between information pieces (e.g., "X and Y are both about cost", "X is related to Y") but the mechanism is unclear; connections identify similarity or co-occurrence but do not explain causation; when probed, provides vague explanations (e.g., "They just seem connected") |
| Level 3 | Makes 3–4 accurate connections showing how pieces of information relate; explains mechanisms or causation (e.g., "X causes Y because..." OR "When X increases, Y decreases, which suggests..."); uses connections to build understanding (e.g., "These three points all suggest that the root issue is Z"); connections are accurate based on information provided |
| Level 4 | Makes 4–5 integrated connections weaving multiple data points into a relational structure; identifies interaction patterns (e.g., constraints → behavior → outcome); demonstrates at least 1 of the following: explicitly maps a causal chain (A→B→C); identifies a feedback loop or cyclical pattern; distinguishes direct vs. indirect relationships (e.g., "X directly causes Y, but only indirectly affects Z through Y") |

---

#### Indicator 2: Recognizes patterns, themes, and root causes

**What counts as a pattern:**
- FREQUENCY: "Multiple customers mentioned [same issue]"
- RELATIONSHIP: "When X occurs, Y also occurs"
- SEGMENT: "Type A wants one thing; Type B wants something different"
- ROOT CAUSE: "These 3 surface issues stem from [underlying cause]"
- TEMPORAL: "Problem worsened over time" or "varies by time of day"

**What does NOT count:**
- Single issue from one person
- List without connections
- Vague theme ("experience needs improvement")

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Identifies 0 patterns or themes; statements are purely descriptive, listing what was heard without interpretation (e.g., "They said X, Y, and Z" without noting these are examples of a pattern); does not distinguish between surface issues and underlying causes |
| Level 2 | Identifies 1–2 emerging patterns with basic labeling (e.g., "Cost is a theme" OR "Time pressure came up a lot"); pattern descriptions are incomplete (names the pattern but doesn't explain what it means or provide supporting examples); does not differentiate root causes from symptoms |
| Level 3 | Identifies 2–3 legitimate patterns with clear, descriptive labels (e.g., "ordering confusion" not just "problems"); supports each pattern with at least one specific example from the conversation; distinguishes between at least one symptom and one plausible root cause |
| Level 4 | Identifies 3–4 legitimate patterns/themes including at least one deeper theme (not just what was said but why it might be happening); supports each pattern with at least one specific example and briefly explains its significance; identifies a root cause with clear rationale distinguishing it from symptoms; demonstrates at least 1 of the following: notes that a pattern appeared repeatedly across different parts of the conversation; identifies a contradiction between two things the interviewee said and offers a tentative explanation (e.g., "Some said X, others said Y — this might mean...") |

---

#### Indicator 3: Synthesizes evidence into insights and implications

> Note: Synthesis is NOT summarizing (restating what was said) or analyzing (identifying patterns). Synthesis involves creating NEW understanding by proposing explanations for why patterns exist and how elements relate causally.

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Provides 0 insights; only restates facts or opinions (e.g., "They said they need more time" without interpreting what this means); lists observations without showing relationships (e.g., "Customers mentioned A, B, and C" without connecting them); no causal reasoning; no implications offered for actions or decision-making |
| Level 2 | Provides 1–2 descriptive patterns with minimal explanation; groups similar observations into categories (e.g., "Three customers mentioned timing issues") but does not explain WHY the pattern exists; attempts 1 tentative causal statement but reasoning is incomplete or circular (e.g., "Users want it easier because it's hard" without specifying mechanisms); implications are generic or vague (e.g., "We should improve it" without specifying what or how) |
| Level 3 | Provides 2–3 thematic insights with explicit causal reasoning; connects 3–4 different observations into a coherent theme (e.g., "Wait times, ordering confusion, and staffing complaints all relate to operational inconsistency"); proposes a 1-level causal explanation for at least one insight (explains WHY the pattern exists using "because" reasoning: "Customers struggle with login because password requirements are complex AND reset process is confusing"); provides specific implications tied to user/client context (e.g., "This suggests we should simplify password requirements OR implement single sign-on"); evidence → insight → implication structure is present for at least one insight |
| Level 4 | Provides 3–4 thematic insights with multi-level causal reasoning for at least one of them; connects 4+ observations into an integrated understanding; for at least one insight, proposes both a cause and explains why that cause exists (e.g., "Customers struggle with the menu because the options aren't grouped logically — and the layout probably hasn't been redesigned as the menu grew"); implications are specific and actionable (not just "improve X" but "specifically do Y because it addresses Z"); addresses at least one piece of evidence that complicates the picture (e.g., "While most said X, one person said Y — this apparent contradiction suggests...") |

---

#### Indicator 4: Organizes insights with clear logical structure

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Presents insights as a disconnected list without organization (e.g., "First insight is X. Also, Y. And another thing is Z."); does not group related insights or show how they connect to each other; no clear beginning, middle, or end to the presentation of findings |
| Level 2 | Attempts to group insights (e.g., puts related ideas together) but grouping logic is unclear or inconsistent (the listener can see that related things are near each other but cannot tell why they belong together); presents 2–3 insights but relationships between them are not explained; has basic structure (beginning and end) but flow between insights is choppy |
| Level 3 | Insights are arranged in a logical sequence where the order serves a clear purpose — most important first, problem before implication, or cause before effect — and the listener can follow why each insight appears where it does (e.g., "The confusion starts before customers even reach the counter, and that leads to the second issue, which is that staff spend time redirecting people instead of serving them."); explains how at least two insights relate to each other (e.g., "These first two insights are related because..." OR "This insight builds on the previous one"); presents insights in a logical sequence with clear transitions |
| Level 4 | Insights form a coherent narrative arc where earlier insights lead logically to later ones; student explicitly states an organizing principle at some point (e.g., "I'll start with what we heard and then explain what it means" OR "I'll organize by moving from symptoms to root causes"); demonstrates at least 1 of the following: shows how insights work together to form a complete picture (e.g., "When you combine insights X and Y, we see that Z"); prioritizes insights by importance or actionability and explicitly states which matters most and why |

### Cluster 3: Decision Framing & Evidence-Based Judgment

**Research Foundation:** Decision framing draws from Tversky & Kahneman's (1981) research on decision framing effects and Hammond, Keeney, & Raiffa's (1999) smart choices framework emphasizing clear articulation of objectives, alternatives, and constraints. Evidence-based reasoning operationalizes Toulmin's (2003) argumentation model and Baron's (2008) research. Uncertainty acknowledgment is grounded in Kahneman & Tversky's (1979) prospect theory and Gigerenzer & Gaissmaier's (2011) work on heuristic decision-making. Recommendation quality aligns with the AAC&U (2009) Critical Thinking VALUE Rubric and Stanovich & West's (2000) research on reasoning quality.

**References**
* Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1981). The framing of decisions and the psychology of choice. *Science, 211*(4481), 453–458.
* Hammond, J. S., Keeney, R. L., & Raiffa, H. (1999). *Smart choices: A practical guide to making better decisions*. Harvard Business School Press.
* Stanovich, K. E., & West, R. F. (2000). Individual differences in reasoning. *Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 23*(5), 645–665.

* Baron, J. (2008). *Thinking and deciding* (4th ed.). Cambridge University Press.

* Gigerenzer, G., & Gaissmaier, W. (2011). Heuristic decision making. *Annual Review of Psychology, 62*, 451–482.

**Indicators**
* Clearly frames the decision or direction.
* Connects recommendations directly to evidence.
* Addresses uncertainty, limitations, trade-offs, or alternative explanations.
* Provides a recommendation that is logical, feasible, and aligned with client needs.

---

#### Indicator 1: Clearly frames the decision or direction

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Does not clearly state what decision, question, or direction is being considered; problem and decision are blended or left implicit (e.g., discusses issues without proposing what should be decided); cannot articulate a clear decision point |
| Level 2 | Partially articulates a decision or direction but the scope, criteria, or alternatives remain vague (e.g., "We need to do something about X" without specifying what options are being considered); does not specify decision criteria; alternatives or options are not mentioned |
| Level 3 | States a clear decision question or set of options (e.g., "We need to choose between A and B, given constraints C and D"); indicates decision criteria or constraints for the decision (e.g., cost, feasibility, user experience); decision framing is complete enough that someone could understand what's being decided and on what basis |
| Level 4 | Precisely frames the decision space including key options, constraints, and at least one success criterion; goes beyond Level 3 by demonstrating at least 1 of the following: explicitly states what is NOT being decided (clarifies scope boundaries); distinguishes between a short-term action and a longer-term consideration; sequences multiple related decisions (e.g., "First we decide X, which then determines options for Y"); identifies who needs to make the decision and when |

---

#### Indicator 2: Connects recommendations directly to evidence

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Makes recommendations without referencing evidence from the conversation (0 explicit connections to stakeholder statements, data, or observations); reasoning appears based on personal preference (e.g., "I think we should do X because I like it") or generic assumptions (e.g., "This is a best practice" without connecting to specific context); when asked "Why this recommendation?" cannot cite specific evidence |
| Level 2 | Cites evidence 1–2 times but connections to recommendations are weak (e.g., mentions "The client said X" but doesn't explain how this supports the recommendation); most recommendations lack explicit evidence support (≤40% of recommendations connected to evidence) |
| Level 3 | Supports 50–70% of recommendations with specific, relevant evidence from the conversation ("Because the client said X, and we observed Y, we recommend…"); makes explicit connection statements showing how evidence leads to recommendation (uses "because," "therefore," "this shows that," "given that"); evidence cited is relevant and accurately represents what was said or observed |
| Level 4 | Supports 65–80% of recommendations with evidence; makes the reasoning chain from evidence → interpretation → recommendation explicit for at least one recommendation (e.g., "The evidence of X suggests Y is happening, which means we should do Z because..."); demonstrates at least 1 of the following: contrasts the recommended option with a weaker alternative using evidence (e.g., "We recommend A over B because evidence X supports A but contradicts B"); acknowledges when evidence is limited and explains how the recommendation accounts for this |

---

#### Indicator 3: Addresses uncertainty, limitations, trade-offs, or alternative explanations

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Treats information as certain and complete with no caveats; does not mention uncertainties, unknowns, risks, limitations, or alternative explanations; when challenged or asked "What if you're wrong?" becomes defensive or dismisses the challenge |
| Level 2 | Vaguely acknowledges that "we might not know everything" but does not specify what is uncertain or how it affects the recommendation; does not explain how uncertainties affect the recommendation; mentions alternatives only if directly asked, and doesn't consider them |
| Level 3 | Identifies 1–2 specific uncertainties, limitations (e.g., missing data, potential bias), or trade-offs; briefly explains how at least one uncertainty might affect decisions (e.g., "If cost is higher than expected, we might need to reconsider"); recognizes at least one plausible alternative explanation or path |
| Level 4 | Identifies 2–3 specific uncertainties or trade-offs with at least a brief explanation of their impact; demonstrates at least 1 of the following: explicitly weighs an alternative and explains why the chosen direction is preferred; suggests a way to reduce uncertainty (e.g., "We could pilot test to validate assumption X"); distinguishes between a critical unknown (that would change the decision) and a minor one; specifies a condition under which the recommendation should be reconsidered |

---

#### Indicator 4: Provides a recommendation that is logical, feasible, and aligned with client needs

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Provides no clear recommendation, OR recommendation is internally inconsistent (contradicts itself), infeasible given stated constraints (e.g., recommends expensive solution when budget was identified as major constraint), or unrelated to client's stated problem; when asked "What should the client do?" cannot provide coherent answer |
| Level 2 | Provides a recommendation but it is generic (e.g., "Improve communication" OR "Do more research" without specifying what to improve or research); recommendation is plausible but lacks specificity needed for action; partially misaligned with constraints or priorities that were clearly stated |
| Level 3 | Provides a specific, actionable recommendation that addresses the core problem identified (e.g., "Implement a weekly check-in process where team members report blockers" rather than just "improve communication"); recommendation is feasible given major constraints mentioned; explains in 2–3 sentences why this recommendation addresses the client's specific need |
| Level 4 | Provides a specific recommendation clearly tailored to the client's context; recommendation follows logically from the analysis presented earlier (explicitly connects back to insights or evidence); demonstrates at least 1 of the following: acknowledges a constraint and explains why the recommendation is still realistic within it; anticipates an implementation consideration (e.g., "This would require X, which seems feasible because Y"); provides brief decision logic (e.g., "We're recommending this over the alternative because...") |

## Professional Agency

### Cluster 1: Professional Self-Efficacy

**Research Foundation:** This cluster operationalizes professional self-efficacy competencies grounded in Bandura's (1977, 1997) Social Cognitive Theory. Communication confidence draws from Bandura's research (effect size d = 0.52) and Mehrabian's (1972) work on nonverbal confidence communication. Clarification-seeking is grounded in Newman's (2002) research on adaptive help-seeking and Zimmerman & Martinez-Pons (1990). Composure operationalizes Bandura's (1997) physiological/affective states dimension and Schwarzer & Jerusalem's (1995) General Self-Efficacy Scale (validated ages 16+, α = .76–.90). Self-assessment accuracy draws from Dunning, Heath, & Suls (2004) and Zimmerman (2000).

**Indicators**
* Communicates with clarity and presence when speaking to adults or stakeholders.
* Asks questions and seeks clarification confidently when uncertain.
* Demonstrates comfort and composure in formal or unfamiliar professional environments.
* Expresses calibrated confidence in ability to perform tasks.

---

#### Indicator 1: Communicates with clarity and presence

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Speaks in a noticeably uncertain tone (barely audible voice) or mumbles unclear words; responses are fragmented or incomplete (trails off mid-sentence ≥3 times, OR provides one-word answers when explanation is appropriate); ideas lack structure (no clear point or explanation — listener cannot understand what is being communicated); requires more than 3 prompts to respond to direct questions |
| Level 2 | Voice is audible but lacks projection or confidence (quiet, hesitant tone); uses noticeable filler language (≥10 filler words per minute: "um," "uh," "like") OR overly deferential language (≥3 instances of "I'm probably wrong but...", "This might be stupid but..."); expresses ideas understandably but structure is inconsistent (mixture of clear statements and rambles); requires 1–2 prompts to continue or complete thoughts |
| Level 3 | Speaks with clear, audible voice appropriate for the setting; uses moderate filler language (5–9 per minute) and no overly apologetic framing; structures ideas logically (states point → provides brief explanation) in 3–5 speaking turns; maintains presence without requiring prompts (volunteers complete responses) |
| Level 4 | Speaks with a steady, confident tone and natural projection; uses minimal filler language (≤5 per minute) and professional framing (no apologetic qualifiers); structures responses clearly in 3–5 speaking turns (point → explanation → implication or example); demonstrates at least 1 of the following: maintains composure and clarity when challenged or interrupted (responds calmly, doesn't become flustered); uses purposeful pauses instead of fillers (pauses to think, then speaks clearly); adjusts pace or emphasis to highlight key points |

---

#### Indicator 2: Asks questions and seeks clarification confidently when uncertain

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Shows 0 instances of clarification or help-seeking when uncertainty is present (proceeds with task despite confusion, OR provides vague/incorrect responses rather than seeking clarification); when directly asked "Do you understand?" says "yes" but subsequent actions show did not understand; avoids engagement when confused (becomes silent, gives up, or waits passively) |
| Level 2 | Asks 1–2 clarification questions but only after prolonged struggle (≥2 minutes of visible confusion before asking) OR only after being directly prompted ("Do you have questions?"); questions are vague (e.g., "I'm not sure" OR "I don't get it" without specifying what is unclear); tone when asking is hesitant or excessively apologetic (e.g., "Sorry, but I'm confused about...") |
| Level 3 | Asks 2–3 specific clarification questions when uncertainty arises without prolonged struggle; questions are task-relevant and targeted (e.g., "What's the deadline for this?" OR "Should I focus on X or Y?" OR "Can you clarify what you mean by Z?"); asks without excessive apology (straightforward: "Can you clarify X?" rather than "I'm sorry, I'm probably being dumb, but...") |
| Level 4 | Asks 3–4 specific clarification questions without prolonged struggle; at least one question demonstrates partial understanding and checks it (e.g., "I understand X, but I'm uncertain whether Y also applies" OR "My understanding is X — is that correct?"); questions are precise enough to get needed information efficiently; demonstrates at least 1 of the following: asks about assumptions or edge cases (e.g., "Are we assuming Y?" OR "What if Z scenario occurs?"); distinguishes between different types of information needed (e.g., "To clarify the scope: are we including X?" vs. "To understand priorities: is Y more important than Z?") |

---

#### Indicator 3: Demonstrates composure in formal or unfamiliar environments

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 – Beginning | Shows 0 instances of composure when facing unfamiliar professional situations (speaking with authority figure, unexpected question, ambiguous instruction, or higher-stakes decision moment); exhibits high anxiety through vocal patterns: voice trembling/shaking ≥3 times, very long hesitation pauses (≥8 seconds before responding to direct questions), or speaking extremely rapidly (attempting to rush through discomfort); withdraws from engagement (stops responding for ≥30 seconds, requests to end conversation, OR says "I don't know" to ≥4 questions without attempting to engage); requires ≥3 reassurances or encouragements from interviewer to continue participation |
| Level 2 – Developing | Shows 1–2 instances of maintained engagement despite discomfort (unexpected question, correction, or increased formality); exhibits moderate anxiety through vocal patterns: nervous laughter ≥3 times, frequent verbal hedging (≥8 instances of "um," "uh," "I mean" in a single response), or noticeable hesitation pauses (3–7 seconds before responding); performance deteriorates when pressure increases (responses become significantly shorter — drops from 30+ words to <10 words, OR clarity decreases noticeably); recovers participation with 1–2 encouragements from interviewer |
| Level 3 – Applying | Shows 3–4 instances of maintained engagement despite discomfort (unexpected question, correction, or increased formality); maintains controlled vocal patterns: minimal voice trembling (≤1 instance), brief hesitation pauses only (1–3 seconds), steady speaking pace; if nervousness appears in voice (slight quaver or faster pace), recovers within 10–15 seconds without external support (pauses, takes audible breath, continues with steady voice); continues participating without withdrawal even when uncertain (attempts response rather than saying "I don't know" and stopping) |
| Level 4 – Mastery | Shows 4–5 instances of maintained engagement despite discomfort; vocal qualities remain broadly consistent across different moments — speaking pace, volume, and clarity stay within a reasonable range whether discussing familiar or novel topics; demonstrates at least 1 of the following: uses an explicit composure strategy when needed (says "Let me think about that for a moment" before responding to a difficult question, OR takes an audible breath before continuing); recovers from an unexpected or challenging moment within 5–7 seconds and continues smoothly; acknowledges discomfort appropriately without losing effectiveness (e.g., "This is a tough question, and here's how I'm thinking about it...") |

---

#### Indicator 4: Expresses calibrated confidence (pre vs. post simulation self-assessment)

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 – Beginning | Pre-task confidence is severely miscalibrated (≥2 levels different from actual performance); post-task self-assessment is also highly inaccurate; cannot identify specific strengths or growth areas (says "I don't know" OR provides only vague, general statements); expresses fixed mindset attributions (e.g., "I'm just not good at this" without referencing effort or strategies) |
| Level 2 – Developing | Pre-task confidence is moderately miscalibrated (1–2 levels different from actual); identifies 1–2 strengths or growth areas but without specific evidence (e.g., "I think my questions were okay" without citing examples); confidence statements don't reference controllable factors (effort, strategies, practice) |
| Level 3 – Applying | Pre-task confidence is reasonably calibrated (within 1 level of actual performance); post-task assessment is similarly accurate; identifies 2–3 specific strengths or growth areas with simulation examples; uses growth-oriented language when discussing performance (e.g., "I could improve...", "I'm working on...", "With more practice..."); references effort or strategies when explaining performance |
| Level 4 – Mastery | Pre-task confidence is well-calibrated (within 1 level) AND post-task assessment is more accurate or shows visible reflection; identifies 2–3 specific strengths and at least 1 growth area, each with concrete simulation evidence; demonstrates at least 1 of the following: explicitly links confidence to specific evidence (e.g., "Confident in questioning because I asked follow-ups on three different topics"); recognizes where confidence changed based on the experience; uses nuanced self-assessment across different skill areas rather than one global self-rating; adjusts confidence based on task experience (if initially overconfident, acknowledges learning; if underconfident, recognizes capability demonstrated) |

### Cluster 3: Growth-Oriented Mindset

**Research Foundation:** This cluster operationalizes growth mindset and adaptive learning beliefs grounded in Dweck's (2006) implicit theories of intelligence research. #### Indicator 1: Interprets feedback as information for improvement

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | **In simulation:** Responds defensively to challenging questions or corrections (argues, provides immediate justifications without considering the point, OR becomes silent/withdrawn). **In reflection:** Describes feedback as personal criticism (e.g., "They thought I wasn't good at it" vs. "They suggested I work on X"); cannot identify any learning gained from feedback received; attributes feedback to evaluator's judgment rather than as information about the work (e.g., "They just didn't like my approach") |
| Level 2 | **In simulation:** Accepts challenging questions without visible defensiveness but engagement is minimal (brief acknowledgment like "okay" without elaboration or adjustment). **In reflection:** Acknowledges receiving feedback but interpretation is vague or emotional (e.g., "I took it okay" OR "It was hard to hear"); mentions 1 instance of using feedback but doesn't explain what specifically changed or why |
| Level 3 | **In simulation:** Responds to challenging questions constructively at least once (acknowledges the point, asks a clarifying question about the feedback, OR adjusts the response based on pushback). **In reflection:** Explicitly reframes at least 1–2 feedback instances as information for improvement (e.g., "When my mentor said my questions were too broad, I learned to ask more specific follow-ups"); links each instance to at least one concrete action taken (what specifically changed); uses learning-oriented language (not "they criticized" but "they helped me see") |
| Level 4 | **In simulation:** Responds constructively to challenging questions in 2–3 instances; in at least one instance goes beyond acceptance — asks a follow-up about the feedback, proposes a revised approach, or explicitly names what they are taking away. **In reflection:** Describes how feedback improved performance using at least one clear causal link (e.g., "Feedback that my rapport was weak → I realized I wasn't building on stakeholder responses → I focused on acknowledgment → my follow-up questions improved"); demonstrates at least 1 of the following: shows meta-awareness (e.g., "I initially felt defensive but then recognized the feedback was valid"); distinguishes which feedback was most useful and why; describes seeking feedback proactively |

#### Indicator 2: Reflects on learning and references concrete examples of skill improvement

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | Cannot identify any examples of improvement or skill development (says "I don't know" OR "Nothing has changed"); describes abilities in static terms only (e.g., "I'm good at X" OR "I'm bad at Y" without temporal comparison); when asked "How have you improved?" provides no before-after evidence or says abilities haven't changed |
| Level 2 | Identifies 1–2 general improvement areas without specific details (e.g., "I've gotten better at communication" without explaining what aspect improved or how); vague temporal comparison (e.g., "I used to be worse" without specifying when or what was different); cannot name what practice, feedback, or experience contributed to improvement |
| Level 3 | Identifies 2–3 specific skills that improved with a clear before-after comparison (e.g., "At the beginning of the program, I only asked closed questions; now I ask more open-ended ones"); names what contributed to at least one improvement (practice, feedback, or a specific learning experience — e.g., "Practicing in mock interviews helped me learn to follow up"); provides at least one concrete example with enough specific detail to be credible |
| Level 4 | Identifies 3–4 specific skills that improved with clear before-after evidence; for at least two improvements, explains what changed and what caused it; demonstrates at least 1 of the following: uses specific metrics or observable evidence to characterize the change (e.g., "I went from asking 2 follow-up questions to 5–6"); connects improvement in one skill to development in another (e.g., "Better listening helped my follow-up questions"); articulates what they are still working on with a forward-looking framing; identifies an improvement pattern across multiple skills |

#### Indicator 3: Responds to setbacks by adjusting strategies rather than disengaging

| Level | Criterion |
| :---- | :---- |
| Level 1 | **In simulation:** When a setback happens, becomes visibly discouraged (long silence ≥15 seconds, gives up on a line of questioning, OR says "I don't know what else to do" without trying an alternative). **In reflection:** Attributes setbacks to fixed ability (e.g., "I'm just not good at this" OR "Some people are naturally better at presentations") without mentioning effort or strategy; describes disengaging after difficulty (e.g., "I'd probably give up" OR "I'd ask someone else to do it"); interprets struggle as evidence of inability |

| Level 4 | **In simulation:** Reframes a setback as information with minimal emotional disruption; tries a different approach without needing encouragement; in at least one instance verbalizes the learning (e.g., "That approach didn't work, which tells me..."). **In reflection:** Provides at least one setback → strategy adjustment → outcome narrative with enough detail to be credible; demonstrates at least 1 of the following: analyzes what didn't work to inform the next attempt (e.g., "I realized my questions were too vague, so I made them more specific"); shows meta-awareness of own resilience growth (e.g., "I used to give up quickly; now I try multiple approaches"); describes increased determination after setbacks |
